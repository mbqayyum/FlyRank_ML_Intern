Lane 2 · Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Which Content Signals Predict Search Decline — and Can ML Prioritize Refreshes Better Than Hand‑Rules?

📄 M. B. Qayyum 📊 30,000 pages · 32 clients 📅 July 2026
Abstract. Content teams managing large portfolios must decide which pages to refresh first when search visibility declines — a decision currently guided by manual audits or blanket age-based rules. Using 30,000 pseudonymized content pages from 32 clients in the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, we trained a Random Forest classifier to identify pages experiencing meaningful search-impression decline (>20% month-over-month). The model achieves a Precision@50 of 0.740 on a held-out client split — a ~3× lift over a transparent hand-rule baseline (0.240) — and an ROC AUC of 0.750 versus the baseline's 0.627. The result is a ranked refresh queue with reason codes and confidence tiers that content strategists can use to allocate editing resources to the pages most likely to benefit from intervention.
Section 1

Introduction & Problem Statement

Imagine a content portfolio of 10,000+ pages. Every month, some pages gain search impressions while others quietly lose ground. An SEO manager must decide: which declining pages are worth the $150–$500 per-article cost of a refresh, and which should be left alone?

The naive approach — "refresh everything older than 180 days" — ignores traffic momentum, engagement quality, keyword opportunity, and content type. It treats a page that is gracefully stable the same as one actively hemorrhaging traffic. The result: wasted editorial budget on pages that don't need attention, and missed recovery windows on pages that do.

Section 3

Methodology

Label Definition

The binary target is_declining_label equals 1 when trend_direction == "down" — i.e., when a page's impressions dropped more than 20% from the previous 30-day window to the most recent 30-day window. This affects 16,262 of 30,000 pages (54.2%), making it a slightly imbalanced but not extreme classification task.

Features

The model uses 18 numeric features and 8 categorical features (one-hot encoded), selected to be safe from label leakage:

Numeric Features
search_volume competition cpc word_count char_count log_impressions_90d log_clicks_90d log_sessions_90d log_ai_sessions_90d days_with_impressions days_with_sessions content_age_days days_since_last_update ctr avg_position engagement_rate scroll_rate ai_traffic_pct
Categorical Features
competition_level content_type main_intent age_tier freshness_tier word_count_tier impression_tier position_tier

Baseline: Transparent Hand-Rule Score

Before training any model, we built a transparent, interpretable baseline — a weighted composite score combining four sub-scores:

ComponentWeightLogic
Visibility Score40%Percentile rank of log(impressions_90d)
Freshness Risk30%Percentile rank of days_since_last_update
Position Opportunity25%(1 − normalized position) × visibility
Depth Gap5%(1 − percentile rank of word_count) × visibility

The baseline also assigns human-readable reason codes (e.g., stale_visible_page, declining_with_demand) — making every recommendation explainable.

Models Trained

  • 🌲
    Random Forest — 200 trees, max depth 10, min 25 samples/leaf, balanced subsample class weights. Captures non-linear feature interactions while resisting overfitting.
  • 📐
    Logistic Regression — StandardScaler → balanced class weights, max 1,000 iterations. A simple linear benchmark to test if the pattern is non-linear.
  • 🌿
    Decision Tree — max depth 5, min 50 samples/leaf, balanced class weights. Single-tree interpretability check.

Validation Design: Client-Holdout Split

We do not use a naive random 80/20 row split. Instead, we hold out ~20% of clients entirely — ensuring no client's pages appear in both train and test. This prevents the model from memorizing client-specific patterns and tests generalization to unseen clients.

Leakage checks performed: (1) Verified that trend_direction and trend_pct are excluded from all feature lists. (2) Trained with trend_pct deliberately included and confirmed it produces near-perfect AUC → leakage confirmed and excluded. (3) Verified no 30-day comparison columns that directly encode the label period are used as features without documented justification.

Results

All models and the baseline are evaluated on the same client-holdout test set. The base rate is 54.2% (majority-class), so any metric must be interpreted against that baseline — a precision of 54% would simply be "guessing the majority class."

Key result: The Random Forest's Precision@50 of 0.740 means that among the top-50 pages flagged for refresh, 74% are genuinely declining — versus only 24% with hand-rules. That's a 3.1× lift. Since the base rate is 54.2%, the model's 74% represents a meaningful 1.37× multiplier even over naive majority-class guessing, and the AUC of 0.750 confirms real discriminative power beyond chance.

Section 5

Limitations & Honest Framing

We state these limitations ourselves, before a reader does — because doing so is what earns trust in applied research.

  • Observational, not causal. The model identifies associations between content signals and decline — it does not prove that refreshing a page causes recovery. A controlled experiment (refreshing a random subset and measuring lift) would be needed for causal claims.
  • This does not "predict Google." We score pages using observed, lagging signals from a fixed time window. The model does not forecast algorithmic changes, core updates, or future search-engine behavior.
  • No guarantee of ROI. A high refresh-priority score means the page shows patterns associated with decline in this dataset. It does not promise that editing the page will recover traffic. External factors — competitor content, intent shifts, seasonality — are outside the model's scope.
  • Cross-client generalization is untested. The model is validated on held-out clients from the same 32-client cohort. Performance on a client in a very different vertical or content strategy should be validated before acting on its scores.
  • Base rate context matters. With 54.2% of pages declining, any metric (precision, accuracy) must be read against that majority-class rate. We report AUC and lift over baseline to isolate real discriminative power from class imbalance.
  • Snapshot, not time-series. The 30k-row starter dataset is a single 90-day cross-section. A temporal validation (training on earlier months, testing on later months) on the full warehouse release would further strengthen confidence.
  • All claims use directional / decision-support language. We say "associated with," "observed," "the model suggests." We do not say "causes," "proves," or "will."

Ranked Recommendations

The following action playbook translates model output into editorial decisions. Each tier is ordered by urgency and expected impact. Start at Tier 1; move down as capacity allows.

1

Refresh & Review CTR

6,657 pages with high impressions but low click-through rates (<0.5%). These pages are visible but not compelling — refreshing titles, meta descriptions, and opening content may recover clicks without needing new ranking positions.

High Priority · 22%
2

Refresh

8,178 pages flagged for general content refresh — declining with demand, model-flagged decline risk, or stale visibility. Update content, add new information, review internal linking, and re-optimize for current search intent.

High Priority · 27%
3

Refresh & Review Engagement

1,990 pages with sufficient sessions (≥30) but low engagement rate (<30%) or low scroll rate (<30%). The traffic arrives, but readers leave quickly. Focus on content quality, page structure, and reader experience.

Review · 7%
4

Expand & Refresh

82 thin pages (<1,200 words) that still have meaningful visibility (≥250 impressions). These are content-depth gaps — expanding them with substantive content may unlock latent ranking potential.

Review · <1%
5

Monitor

13,093 pages that don't trigger urgent action signals. Continue monitoring — re-score monthly to catch new declines early. These pages are either stable, growing, or too low-visibility to justify refresh investment now.

Monitor · 44%
How to use this: Treat the queue as a reviewer aid, not an automatic publishing decision. The safest first production use is to inspect high-confidence rows, verify the page manually, compare the recommendation against editorial context, and then act. The reason codes tell you why — use them.
